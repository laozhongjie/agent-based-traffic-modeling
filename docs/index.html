Agent-Based Modeling of Traffic Congestion on Road Networks

How does free-flowing traffic tip into a jam? We simulate every vehicle on a ring road and on the real road network of Singapore's Bugis district, locate the critical density at which congestion sets in, and test whether planning changes can move it.

RQ1 · Ring road

Jams without a bottleneck

On a single-lane ring with no traffic lights, free flow breaks into stop-and-go waves that travel backwards once the density exceeds a critical value. We compare where this happens for the NaSch, OVM and IDM models.

RQ2 · City network

A network-wide critical density

≈ 13 veh/km per lane

On the Bugis network, with its junctions and traffic lights, the network mean speed falls to half its near-empty value at about 13 vehicles per kilometre of lane, and the flow levels off near 170 veh/h per lane.

RQ3 · Planning

A closure hurts; a new road changes little

ρc −3.4% after a closure

Closing the most central removable road segment lowers the critical density from 13.35 to 12.90 veh/km and pushes congestion onto the neighbouring streets. A new 148 m link changes the network speed by less than the seed-to-seed spread: Braess's paradox appears only next to the link.

RQ1 · Ring road

Jams without a bottleneck

A single-lane ring of 1000 m with no traffic lights, so congestion can only come from the interaction between vehicles. In the IDM density sweep below (one run per density, 1800 s, the first 600 s discarded), the flow rises with density up to about 30 veh/km and then falls as stop-and-go waves form. The hero animation at the top of the page shows the same transition for the NaSch model.

The NaSch and OVM sweeps and the critical density of each model will be added here when the ring-road analysis is final.

RQ2 · City network

A network-wide critical density

The road network of Singapore's Bugis district, imported from OpenStreetMap: 1,129 roads, 669 junctions, 78 of them with traffic lights, and 179.7 km of car lanes. A controller keeps the number of cars fixed by giving each car a new random destination before it arrives. Each density was run with three random seeds.

The replay of the SUMO runs loads when you scroll here.

Does network structure predict where jams start?

Only weakly. Edge betweenness, the number of shortest routes through a road, correlates with the density at which the road first congests with a Spearman coefficient of about −0.3. Once the traffic load and the number of lanes are taken into account, the correlation disappears.

RQ3 · Planning

Moving the critical point

Three changes to the baseline, each in one place: closing a segment of Victoria Street, the most central road segment that can be removed; removing one lane in each direction on the same segment; or adding a 148 m two-way link between the Republic Avenue ramp and Beach Road. Each network is swept over the same densities and seeds.

Seeds 1–3; for the baseline and the new link, seeds 1–10 at 25–35 veh/km. Only the closure moves the critical density by more than the seed-to-seed spread.

One simulator for every experiment

All runs use one build of SUMO 1.28.0 compiled from source. NaSch and IDM are built into SUMO; we implemented the optimal velocity model in C++ and added it to the same build, so ring and city results can be compared directly.

Every run is recorded and checked

Each run keeps its configuration, random seed, SUMO version and code commit. Automatic checks for collisions, missing vehicles and teleports decide whether a run enters the result tables.

Model equations

NaSch

\[\begin{aligned} v_i &\leftarrow \min(v_i+1,\ v_{\max}) \\ v_i &\leftarrow \min(v_i,\ g_i) \\ v_i &\leftarrow \max(v_i-1,\ 0) \\ x_i &\leftarrow x_i+v_i \end{aligned}\]

Accelerate, keep a safe gap, slow down at random with probability \(p\) (third line), then move. \(g_i\): empty cells ahead. All vehicles update in parallel.

Explore this model

OVM

\[\frac{dv_i}{dt}=\alpha\left[V(s_i)-v_i\right]\]\[V(s)=v_{\max}\,\frac{\tanh\frac{s-s_c}{d}+\tanh\frac{s_c}{d}}{1+\tanh\frac{s_c}{d}}\]

\(s_i\): gap to the car ahead; \(\alpha\): sensitivity; \(s_c\), \(d\): where \(V\) is steepest and how wide the transition is. Uniform flow becomes unstable when \(V'(s) > \alpha/2\).

Explore this model

IDM

\[\frac{dv_i}{dt}=a\left[1-\left(\frac{v_i}{v_0}\right)^{\delta}-\left(\frac{s^*(v_i,\Delta v_i)}{s_i}\right)^{2}\right]\]\[s^*(v,\Delta v)=s_0+\max\!\left(0,\ vT+\frac{v\,\Delta v}{2\sqrt{ab}}\right)\]

\(s_i\): gap to the car ahead; \(\Delta v_i\): approach rate; \(v_0,\ T,\ s_0,\ a,\ b,\ \delta\): desired speed, time gap, minimum gap, maximum acceleration, comfortable deceleration and exponent.
