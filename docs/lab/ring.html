RQ1 · Ring-road experiment

Phase transition on a highway

Find the critical density at which free flow breaks down on a single-lane ring road without traffic lights, and tell spontaneous jams apart from jams caused by a bottleneck.

Question Method Results Data

Question

  1. On a road without traffic lights, can the transition from free flow to congestion be described as a phase transition? What is the critical density?
  2. How does the critical density depend on the vehicle model (NaSch, OVM, IDM), the level of randomness and the system size?
  3. Can jams form spontaneously without any bottleneck, and how do they differ, in shape and statistics, from jams caused by a bottleneck?
  4. Does the simulated critical density agree with real highway data?

Method

GoalFind the critical density for the free-flow-to-jam transition on a road without traffic lights; test how it depends on the model, randomness and system size; distinguish spontaneous from bottleneck-induced congestion.
SettingClosed single-lane ring road in SUMO. Vehicles neither enter nor leave, and there are no traffic lights.
ModelsNaSch, OVM and IDM, all run in SUMO.
Control variables
  1. Vehicle density (veh/km): swept from low to high, with finer steps near the transition.
  2. System size: at least three ring lengths.
  3. Randomness: the NaSch slowdown probability \(p\).
  4. Bottleneck: none, or a reduced-speed section (a work zone, tunnel or similar capacity drop). A traffic light on the ring is an optional bridging case.
Held fixedEach experiment set changes only one control variable. All other parameters, the simulation length, the warm-up period and the seed set stay the same.
MeasurementsMean speed, flow at a fixed point, order parameter (fraction of jammed vehicles) and its fluctuations, jam locations, jam-wave speed.

  • B Guowei2.   • A Zhongjie3. OVM against theory (question 1): mark on the density axis the range where the instability condition \(V'(h) > a/2\) holds, then mark the simulated critical density and check that it falls inside.
  • B Guowei4.   • E Hongfang5. Against real data (question 4): plot the simulated fundamental diagram and PeMS detector data (per lane) on the same axes, and compare the maximum flow and the critical density in magnitude.

Why only a ring road?

  1. Density can be set exactly. The ring is closed: no vehicle enters or leaves, so the density stays at \(N/L\) and can be stepped like a temperature. On an open road the density follows the inflow and outflow and drifts over time.
  2. There is no built-in bottleneck. Every point on the ring is alike, so any jam must grow from small disturbances between vehicles, which is spontaneous congestion. On an open road the entrance and exit disturb the flow themselves, so the source of a jam is unclear.
  3. Results can be checked against theory. The exact NaSch fundamental diagram at \(p = 0\) and the OVM instability condition \(V'(h) > a/2\) are both derived for a road closed on itself, so only a ring compares with them directly.
  4. The bottleneck goes on the same ring. Adding one reduced-speed section and changing nothing else lets the two kinds of congestion be compared directly.

“Only a ring road” does not mean a single ring: the system-size sweep uses at least three ring lengths. The cost is that the ring has one lane, no ramps and no lane changes, unlike a real highway. Comparisons with real data therefore use only per-lane fundamental diagrams, which describe local flow and density, not the whole road.

Telling the two kinds of congestion apart: what to expect

The table lists what earlier studies and theory lead us to expect. It is the standard we test against, not a result of this project. If the simulations do not show a feature, we report that.

What to look atExpected for spontaneous congestionExpected for bottleneck-induced congestion
Where jams appearRandom; varies between seedsAlways just upstream of the bottleneck
Space–time diagramJam bands drift backwards (observed on real roads at about 15–20 km/h)Jam bands stay in place; behind a traffic light they grow and shrink with each cycle
Fundamental diagramMaximum flow is set by the driving rulesMaximum flow drops to the bottleneck capacity, and the critical density is lower

Jams without bottlenecks have been observed experimentally: Sugiyama et al., 2008.

How the results are read

Criteria

  • Does the mean speed drop noticeably?
  • Does the flow reach a peak and then decline?
  • Does the order parameter rise sharply, and do its fluctuations peak?
  • Does the space–time diagram show persistent jams or backward-moving waves?
  • For OVM, does the simulated critical density fall within the instability range from linear stability analysis?
  • With a bottleneck, are jams pinned upstream of it, or drifting backwards elsewhere?

Interpretation

If all three models show a flow peak, a rising order parameter and a fluctuation peak at some density, and the transition sharpens as the ring grows, the transition has the hallmarks of a phase transition. If the bottleneck lowers the maximum flow, shifts the critical density down and pins jams just upstream of it, congestion under that condition is bottleneck-dominated.

Minimum convincing experiment

The same ring road at many densities, with all three models, several system sizes and multiple seeds, run with and without a bottleneck.

Common pitfall

Showing only an animation; or seeing jams in a signalized network and calling them a density-driven phase transition.
