RQ2 · City-network experiment

City network

Find the network-level critical density of a real urban district imported from OpenStreetMap, and watch local jams grow into congested regions.

Question Method Results Data

Question

  1. As the average network density rises, is there a network-level critical density at which total network flow peaks and then falls? What is it?
  2. Beyond the critical density, do jams on single links join into large congested regions?
  3. How does the network structure (bottleneck links, edge betweenness) set the critical density and where congestion starts?
  4. How does the network critical density differ from the highway critical density?

Method

GoalFind the network-level critical density of a city district, follow how congestion spreads from single links into large regions, and test how the network structure sets the critical point and where jams appear.
SettingA city district imported from OpenStreetMap; the pipeline is first tested on a 4×4 grid. Intersections, lane counts and speed limits come from the map data. Signals get fixed timings from one shared rule and are not varied in this experiment.
ModelsIDM, run in SUMO; NaSch and OVM as optional robustness checks.
Control variablesNetwork density (vehicles in the network ÷ total lane length, veh/km per lane): swept from low to high, with finer steps near the transition. A vehicle that reaches its destination gets a new random one, so the density stays constant during each run.
Held fixedThe network, signal plans, destination rule, routing rule, seed set and simulation length.
MeasurementsNetwork mean flow and mean speed, share of stopped vehicles, speed and queue length on every link, queue spillbacks, size of the largest congested cluster, and whether the network locks up.
Outputs
  • C Yixiang1. Macroscopic fundamental diagram (question 1): network density \(k\) on the x-axis, network mean flow \(q\) on the y-axis, one set of points per seed. The density at the flow peak is the network critical density.
  • C Yixiang2. Largest congested cluster (question 2): density on the x-axis, the share of road length in the largest congested cluster on the y-axis. Check whether it grows sharply near the critical density.
  • C Yixiang3. Link congestion maps (questions 2, 3): three maps of the network at low, near-critical and high density, with links coloured by mean speed, showing where congestion starts and how it joins up.
  • C Yixiang4. Betweenness against congestion onset (question 3): a scatter plot with one point per link, weighted edge betweenness on the x-axis and the time the link becomes congested on the y-axis. The correlation shows whether betweenness predicts where congestion starts.
  • E Hongfang5. Network against highway (question 4): the network critical density and the highway critical density from the ring-road experiment, each with its error range, on the same density axis.
  • E Hongfang6. Against real data (question 3): mark on one map the links most often congested in the simulations and the slowest links in LTA speed data, and compare how much they overlap. Applies only if the district is in Singapore.

The tag names who makes each figure: whoever runs an experiment makes its figures; comparisons across experiments or with real data are E's.

What to expect

Earlier studies lead us to expect that congestion in a city network is dominated by intersection bottlenecks, so the network critical density should be lower than the highway value; the gap reflects the two congestion mechanisms. This is an expectation to test, not a result of this project. For the macroscopic fundamental diagram and its empirical evidence, see Geroliminis & Daganzo, 2008.

How the results are read

Criteria

  • Does the network mean flow reach a peak and then decline (the peak of the macroscopic fundamental diagram)?
  • Does the share of stopped vehicles keep rising?
  • Do slow links form a connected region that grows quickly?
  • Does the network lock up, with vehicles unable to move for a long time?
  • Do links with high betweenness become congested earlier?

Interpretation

If the network flow peaks at some density and then falls, while the largest congested region grows quickly near that density, there is a network-level transition. How closely edge betweenness tracks the congestion onset tests whether a network measure can predict where the bottlenecks are.

Minimum convincing experiment

A density sweep on a real city district, with several seeds at each density.

Common pitfall

Sweeping the demand entering the network without controlling how many vehicles are inside, which gives no network density; or using only a regular grid, which does not represent a city.
