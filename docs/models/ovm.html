Optimal Velocity Model (OVM)

Each driver reads the gap to the car ahead, decides how fast that gap allows, and catches up with that speed only gradually. No randomness is needed: if drivers respond too slowly, uniform traffic turns into stop-and-go by itself.

The rule

Positions and speeds are continuous. A car looks at one thing only: its net gap \(s_i\) to the car ahead, from its front bumper to the leader's rear bumper, in metres. The optimal-velocity function \(V(s)\) says how fast that gap allows, and the car moves its speed towards it at rate \(\alpha\):

\[\frac{dx_i}{dt}=v_i,\qquad \frac{dv_i}{dt}=\alpha\,\bigl[V(s_i)-v_i\bigr]\] \[V(s)=v_{\max}\,\frac{\tanh\bigl((s-s_c)/d\bigr)+\tanh(s_c/d)}{1+\tanh(s_c/d)}\]
  • \(V(0)=0\): with no gap the driver wants to stop; for large gaps \(V\to v_{\max}\).
  • \(s_c\) is where the curve is steepest and \(d\) sets how wide the transition is. Near \(s_c\), a small change in gap asks for a large change in speed.
  • \(\alpha\) is in s\(^{-1}\); \(1/\alpha\) is the relaxation time: the gap between actual and desired speed shrinks by a factor \(e\) every \(1/\alpha\) seconds. It is not a reaction delay, but it acts like one: the car always lags behind its target.

OVM uses the gap alone, not the leader's speed. Even if the leader brakes hard, the follower does not slow down until the gap has actually shrunk. This is why OVM cars can collide.

“Optimal” is just the name of \(V\); nobody optimizes anything. Bando et al. measure headway (including the car length) in scaled units. Our implementation and this page use the net gap \(s\) and SI units.

What to look for

  1. Low density: disturbances die out

    At 20 veh/km the gap is 45 m, on the flat part of \(V(s)\), so \(V'(s_0)<\alpha/2\). At 10 s the outlined car stops dead; the cars behind it slow down less and less, and the flow recovers.

  2. Middle density: jams without any randomness

    At 50 veh/km the gap is 15 m, where \(V(s)\) is steepest, so \(V'(s_0)>\alpha/2\). The cars start evenly spaced with only 0.5 m of jitter. Press nothing: within a minute the jitter grows into stop-and-go waves that travel backwards (dark bands in the space–time diagram). Watch “Smallest gap” as well: it turns negative because OVM cars collide.

  3. Faster response: no jams

    Same density with \(\alpha=2.5\ \mathrm{s^{-1}}\). Now \(\alpha/2\) is above the largest slope of \(V\), the unstable band disappears from the fundamental diagram, and a stopped car causes only a brief dip. Drag \(\alpha\) down slowly and watch the band open up.

Why uniform flow breaks down

With every gap equal to \(s_0=L/N-\ell\) and every speed equal to \(V(s_0)\), nobody accelerates: uniform flow solves the equations. Whether it survives depends on a chain reaction. If the leader slows a little, the follower's gap shrinks by \(\Delta s\) and its target speed drops by \(V'(s_0)\,\Delta s\). The follower needs about \(1/\alpha\) seconds to reach the new target; by then the gap has shrunk further, so it brakes harder than its leader did. Each car passes on a larger disturbance than it received.

Linearizing the equations for small gap disturbances \(y_n=s_n-s_0\) gives \(\ddot y_n+\alpha\dot y_n=\alpha V'(s_0)(y_{n+1}-y_n)\). For long waves along the platoon, a disturbance grows when

\[V'(s_0)>\frac{\alpha}{2}\]

\(V'\) measures how strongly a change in gap asks the speed to change; \(\alpha/2\) measures how fast the driver can follow. \(V'\) is largest at \(s=s_c\) (1.13 s\(^{-1}\) here), so the unstable densities form a band around \(s_0=s_c\), and \(\alpha>2.26\ \mathrm{s^{-1}}\) makes every density stable. That band is the shaded region in the demo.

Two limits of this result: it is a long-wave criterion, so on a finite ring and with a finite time step the threshold moves a little (growth_rate in src/traffic_project/ovm.py computes the exact rate). And it only says whether a disturbance starts to grow; once large, the waves settle into steady stop-and-go.

In SUMO

SUMO has no OVM, so we added carFollowModel="OVM" ourselves (sumo/ovm/, attributes alpha, sc, d). Each step of length \(\Delta t\) it computes \(v'=\max\bigl(0,\ v+\Delta t\,\alpha[V(s)-v]\bigr)\), then \(x'=x+\Delta t\,v'\); there are no acceleration limits and collisions are allowed. \(s\) is SUMO's gap, which excludes minGap. The demo uses the same update with \(\Delta t=0.1\) s, so it shows what SUMO does, including the overlaps.

The step length matters: with \(\Delta t=1\) s and \(\alpha=1\) the update becomes \(v'=V(s)\), the lag disappears and much of the instability with it. OVM runs therefore use 0.1 s.
